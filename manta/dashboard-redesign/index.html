Redesigning the Dashboard Around One Person

A deep dive into Principle 1 of The Year Manta Became a Companion. When the whole company is moving fast, the most useful thing a designer can do is name the one person every decision answers to. Here's how designing the dashboard for her changed almost everything.

Case study · Manta Cares

The Problem

Our old dashboard was designed the way most dashboards are: as a place to put everything. Every tool got a tile. Every feature got equal weight. It was, in a sense, fair — and for our actual user, completely overwhelming.

I made a decision early in the year that I'd defend in every design review afterward: we would stop designing for an average user and design for one specific, named person. Meet Carol — a stand-in for our real design persona: she's 64, was diagnosed a few weeks ago, lives an hour from her cancer center, and isn't especially comfortable with technology. Her daughter set up her account. She opens the app on her phone, usually with reading glasses on, sometimes with that same daughter sitting next to her.

When you design the dashboard for Carol, the problems become obvious. A grid of eight tools isn't powerful — it's a quiz she'll fail. Body text sized for a 28-year-old product designer is unreadable to her. A flow that assumes she remembers her way back from three taps deep is a trap. And nowhere on the screen did the product acknowledge the one thing consuming her entire life: she has cancer, and she doesn't know what happens next.

The Approach

Rather than redesign in Figma alone, I worked through the problem in HTML mockups I could open on an actual phone and hand to people, because the gap between "looks fine on a desktop artboard" and "works for Carol at arm's length in bad lighting" is enormous. Each round answered one question: what would make this screen calmer for her? Four principles came out of that work.

Principle 1

Subtract Until It's Calm

The first job was to reduce cognitive load, and the primary tool for that is removal, not addition.

I rebuilt the typographic hierarchy around legibility for older eyes — bigger base type, more line spacing, fewer competing sizes — and leaned on our brand type, Bricolage Grotesque and Inter, to feel warm and human rather than clinical. More importantly, I gave every screen one obvious primary action and demoted everything else. The dashboard stopped trying to show Carol her whole world at once and started answering a simpler question: what should I look at right now?

This same instinct drove work across the product: a simple, on-brand registration flow for new users instead of a generic form, treating sign-up conversion as a design problem, and a content revamp of our educational "drawers" so the information itself was scannable, not a wall of text.

Principle 2

Make It Personal, Because Her Situation Is

A generic dashboard says "here are some tools." Carol doesn't want tools; she wants to know where she is and what's next. So the redesign made the experience personal in the literal sense — it reflects her diagnosis, her stage of the journey, and her next step.

This is where the dashboard connects to the two biggest bets of the year. We made Cancer Maps the spine of the experience, so the dashboard could orient her — you are here — instead of presenting a flat menu. And we brought Hope, our AI companion, onto the dashboard itself, so the most personal possible help — a plain-language answer to whatever is scaring her today — is the first thing available, not something buried.

Principle 3

Design for Two People, Not One

A detail that's easy to miss unless you've sat with this population: Carol rarely uses the app alone. Her daughter set it up, her husband reads it with her, a friend checks in. The care partner is a second, invisible user — and most products pretend they don't exist.

So the redesign explicitly accounts for the person beside her. Flows are written so that someone else can set things up, read along, and help without hijacking Carol's own account or confusing her about whose information is whose. This care-partner lens shows up again, even more concretely, in the appointment experience, where the care partner often becomes the official note-taker.

Principle 4

Build Accessibility In, Not On

For this audience, accessibility isn't a compliance checkbox at the end — it's the difference between a usable product and an unusable one. So I treated it as a foundation.

That meant a deliberate color system built to WCAG contrast standards across both light and dark mode, including the easy-to-neglect states — the error and success colors that have to stay legible and unambiguous for someone who may have low vision or be using the app in a bright kitchen or a dim waiting room. It meant generous touch targets for less precise taps, and language plain enough to never require a second read. None of this is flashy. All of it is what makes the calm in Principle 1 actually hold up in the real world.

Conclusions

Designing the dashboard around Carol didn't just produce a nicer screen — it gave the whole team a shared decision-making tool. "Would this work for Carol?" settled more debates this year than any amount of opinion could have.

  • We replaced a flat tool-grid with a calm, legible, journey-aware dashboard built around one clear action at a time.
  • We made the experience personal, anchoring it to Maps and putting Hope's help first.
  • We designed for the care partner as a real, recurring second user.
  • We treated accessibility as a foundation, with a WCAG-grounded color system across light and dark mode.
