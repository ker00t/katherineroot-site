The Year Manta Became a Companion

A year ago, Manta Cares was a website you visited. Today it's an app that walks beside you. This is the story of how we turned a library of cancer content into a companion for the scariest year of someone's life — and the design decisions that made the transformation hold together.

Case study · Manta Cares

The Starting Point

In May 2025, Manta Cares did a lot of things well. We had real, trustworthy, oncologist-reviewed content. We had Cancer Maps, a Symptom Tracker, an Appointment Notebook. We had a community. But all of it lived on the web, and all of it asked the same thing of the user: know what you're looking for, and go find it.

That's a reasonable ask for a healthy person with time and a steady internet connection. It is a brutal ask for the person we actually serve. As Head of Design, the single most clarifying thing I did this year was refuse to design for a generic "user" and instead design for one specific person: a woman in her sixties, recently diagnosed, living somewhere rural, not especially confident with technology, often with a spouse or adult child reading over her shoulder. She is frightened, exhausted, and holding a folder of paperwork she doesn't understand.

When you hold that person in your mind, the old product's problems stop being abstract. A homepage full of options isn't "robust" — it's a wall. A web-only experience isn't "accessible everywhere" — it's one more thing to log into on a laptop she doesn't open much. A library of great content isn't a resource — it's a search box she has to be well enough to use.

The Bet

I came into the year convinced that showing would beat telling, so rather than argue for a redesign in the abstract, the design team set a direction the whole company could rally behind: Manta should stop being a place you go to and become a presence that comes with you.

That meant three structural bets, each of which became a year-long thread of work:

  • Get on the phone. Meet her where she actually is — a native app in her pocket, not a tab on a laptop.
  • Give her a guide, not a menu. An AI companion, Hope, that meets her in plain language instead of making her navigate.
  • Give the journey a shape. Reorganize the entire experience around Cancer Maps, so a nonlinear, terrifying process feels like a path you can see.

Everything we shipped this year ladders up to one of those bets. What follows are the four design principles that kept them coherent.

Principle 1

Design for the Most Overwhelmed Person in the Room

Every decision this year got measured against her — the newly diagnosed, rural, tech-wary patient with a care partner beside her. If a flow was confusing for her, it failed, no matter how elegant it was for everyone else.

This principle did its quietest, most important work in the unglamorous places. We rebuilt the front door: the login, sign-up, and reset-password pages were rewritten on a real design system, and new users now get a simple, on-brand registration flow instead of a generic form. We treated sign-up conversion as a design problem, not just a marketing one. And we went back through our core educational content — the "drawers" — and revamped them, shipping a content revamp and then ten rebuilt drawers into production for testing.

The throughline was subtraction. The most useful thing a designer can do for an overwhelmed person is decide for them what matters right now, and quietly remove the rest. We leaned on our brand to do emotional work here too — the navy, goldenrod, burgundy, and powder-blue palette and the warmth of Bricolage Grotesque were chosen to feel like a calm, competent human, not a clinical database.

This is the thread I go deeper on in a dedicated case study: the persona-driven redesign of the dashboard, where I show the full before/after and the specific typography, hierarchy, and personalization changes we made for this exact person.

Principle 2

Trade the Library for a Companion

The biggest shift of the year was philosophical: we stopped expecting the user to navigate, and instead built Hope, an AI companion, to do the navigating with her.

What's worth noticing is how deliberately we built her. Hope didn't arrive as a chatbot bolted onto a homepage. We started narrow — selected members chatting with an AI when logged in, a true v0 — and then spent the year making her trustworthy enough to deserve a place in someone's cancer care. We gave her the user's context so she didn't ask people to repeat their diagnosis. We obsessed over her persona, tone, and level of information — because the difference between a helpful guide and a frightening one, for this audience, is entirely a matter of tone. We let her save and retrieve conversations, gave her memory so users get continuity without retelling their story, and then — critically — made it possible for users to see and understand what Hope knows about them, because a companion you can't audit isn't trustworthy.

Then we wove her through the entire product. Hope moved onto the dashboard and into the content drawers. We architected her cleanly — a "dumb" component backed by a smart API — so she could appear anywhere without becoming a tangle. And we made "Dive Deeper with Hope" an offer throughout the app, so no matter where a user got stuck, the guide was one tap away. By the time we gave her a focused, mobile-optimized chat experience and the ability to connect users to actual Manta features, Hope had stopped being a feature and become the connective tissue of the whole product.

The design challenge here wasn't "make a chat UI." It was making an AI feel like a calm, knowledgeable friend to someone in crisis — and giving her a consistent home across a dozen surfaces without her feeling intrusive.

Principle 3

Give a Nonlinear Journey a Map

A cancer journey doesn't move in a straight line, but the human brain desperately wants one. The most ambitious structural decision of the year was to make Cancer Maps the organizing metaphor for the entire product, not just one feature among many.

This was a genuine reorientation. We built the content management system behind Maps so we could create and maintain map content at scale, and then we made the call that defined the back half of the year: the user experience is focused on Maps. Instead of a dashboard of disconnected tools, the product became a navigable path — here's where you are, here's what tends to come next, here's what you can do about it. We've kept refining the navigation since, right up through a round of map UX fixes this June.

The design insight is simple but, I think, important: for a frightening, branching experience, a map is more reassuring than a list. A list says "here are your options." A map says "you are not lost." For our person, that difference is everything.

Principle 4

Win the Appointment

If you ask our users to name the single hardest moment, many will describe the same thing: a rushed visit with an oncologist, often under ten minutes, where they forget their questions, don't understand the answers, and walk out unsure what just happened. Designing for that moment — the highest-stakes few minutes in the whole journey — became its own thread.

We built the loop in three parts. Before the visit, Hope now helps users prepare for appointments with personalized guidance, and that prep arrives as a structured, interactive widget right in the chat. During and after, we shipped Notes — users can now create, edit, and delete their own notes — and made it possible to save a Hope response straight into a note, so the useful thing she said doesn't evaporate. We also shipped document upload, so the folder of paperwork can come into the app and become something Hope can help make sense of.

The design goal across all of it was continuity: prep, capture, and recall should feel like one connected ritual, not three separate tools. The care partner has a real role here too — often they're the one taking notes — and the experience is designed to support that second set of hands.

This loop is the second deep dive: the appointment experience, including the voice-to-text capture work and the prep template we designed for real, time-constrained clinical visits.

Conclusions

A year ago this was a website. Today it's a native app on iOS and Android — we went from no app, to internal TestFlight builds, to inviting real testers, to shipping into the App Store and the Google Play Store, to a steady cadence of usability fixes informed by watching real people use it. Underneath the visible work, we also stood up the foundations that let design make good decisions — end-to-end product analytics, a HIPAA-compliant data dashboard, and a formal qualitative user-interview program — so we're now designing from evidence, not just instinct.

What I'm proudest of isn't any single screen. It's that the strategy actually shifted, and the design held it together:

  • We moved the company from a content-library mindset to a companion mindset, and Hope is now woven through the entire product rather than parked in a corner.
  • We reorganized the whole experience around Cancer Maps, giving a nonlinear journey a shape people can navigate.
  • We got Manta onto the phone, on-brand and accessible, and built the appointment loop that addresses our users' hardest moment.
  • We did all of this anchored to one specific person, which is the discipline that kept a year of fast-moving work from fragmenting.
